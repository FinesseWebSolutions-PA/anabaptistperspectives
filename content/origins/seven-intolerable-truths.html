Episode 03      |    Michael Sattler, Schleitheim Confession

Seven Intolerable Truths

Michael Sattler & The Schleitheim Confession: The Peaceful Revolution Takes Shape

62 min

1525 – 1527

St. Peter’s Monastery, Germany; Strasbourg, France; Schleitheim, Switzerland; Rottenburg, Germany

About This Episode

Episode three takes you on a journey. Starting at a cathedral in the Black Forest, you will follow the life of Michael Sattler. When the Peasants’ War forced him from the monastery, he began a journey that leads us to the other major focus of this episode: the Schleitheim Confession.

In the little town of Schleitheim, Switzerland, early Anabaptist leaders gathered to wrestle through their beliefs. What made them different from the Catholic and Protestant churches around them, and why were they willing to suffer and die for these differences?

As a vivid illustration of how revolutionary these ideas were, you will end the episode in a field at Michael Sattler’s memorial. There you will hear the brutal story of his trial and martyrdom, which he suffered because he refused to compromise his beliefs in Scripture.

Anabaptist Origins is a 10-part documentary series produced by Anabaptist Perspectives that tells the story of the first hundred years of the Anabaptist movement.

And we don’t know for sure who wrote it, but it does have the flavor of other Sattler writings. He was very well educated, and I think he saw clearly what the problems were.

    I remember when I read this the first time, I was asking myself, how did they know to hit these things? And I think when most people come here, the first thing they think is, this thing is tiny. It’s like, that’s it? So you think about how it spread around, and you took this and put it in your pocket and were able to hide when things were the way they were.

    33:50  |  The Foundation for What Followed

    This became, directly or indirectly, the foundation for all of the important confessions, whether Hutterite, or eventually with Menno up in the north. This becomes the thing that shapes what today we often call the Evangelical Anabaptists, and those are the only ones that survived from the Reformation, and that’s where we come from.

    And to have something so early in the movement that has stuck with us even now. I read it for the first time recently, and I was thinking, wow, this is really good. This really nails some things. They’re only two years in. The Anabaptists have barely been around for two years. And they’re already really articulating some fundamental concepts that we very much still read today. And the things they leave out: they’re agreeing with the Catholics and the Protestants. They’re not disagreeing. That’s right.

    I teach a class at Faith Builders where we read this kind of thing, and one of my students once wrote, this is orthodox and interesting stuff. And it is, but we don’t know it. So my plea would be, read some of this stuff.

    35:10  |  The Seven Articles: An Outline

    Could you give us a broad outline of what it’s even about? What is Schleitheim about?

    These seven things have two points to them. One is, this is where we disagree with the Catholics and Protestants. The other is, in the confusion of the beginning, this is where some of the people who want to be Anabaptists, who have been rebaptized, have made some mistakes.

    The order is important. First of all, there’s baptism. That’s how you get into the church. That’s how you receive the grace of God through Jesus. The next one is the ban. And they also say, when they write about the ban, that it should be done just before communion. So baptism brings us into the church. There may be a time when someone needs to be excommunicated. That’s the discipline. And baptism plants you into a new community of love. That’s the breaking of bread. So that’s one, two, three: baptism, ban, breaking of bread.

    And so what do these people do? They separate themselves from the evils of the world. That’s four. Five, pastors in the church. And then two things that apply to everyone who is baptized and part of the church. Do we take up the sword? This was a huge question at the time. And then, Jesus and James said, don’t swear. That’s one that came up all the time, because the government almost always required an oath.

    36:37  |  One: Baptism

    So let’s go through each of those in a little more detail. Why were these particular seven chosen? What were they communicating? What’s the context around it? Let’s go back to baptism. Point number one, baptism.

    Well, it says something very beautiful. This baptism is for the person who is willing to walk in the resurrection. And they’re not claiming perfection. They’re saying, God gives us grace if we come into the body of Christ. And that’s our intention, to walk in the resurrection.

    Again, this idea of faith being required. Not an empty sacrament, not something you’re just doing as a ritual that automatically guarantees it. But is there at least some kind of faith in a person that makes them ask and receive this? They’re not looking at salvation as a one-time entry into the club, where you either did this as a baby or you did it as an adult, you said the sinner’s prayer, you did whatever. It’s those who are signing up to walk in the resurrection of Jesus Christ, the power of the resurrection, death to yourself, alive in Christ Jesus, and literally walking in there. I agree with you. I think that’s a beautiful statement.

    37:42  |  Two: The Ban

    Well, then that takes us right naturally into point number two, the ban.

    The very fact they have a ban says they’re not expecting that anyone’s going to be living a perfect life. They’re recognizing there may be a need to discipline someone. Anabaptism is about discipling, but sometimes you come to the place where you have to discipline. So it’s not to make a perfect church, a church where no one is sinning. It’s to make a church where everyone’s desire is to walk in the resurrection.

    Maybe I’m getting us off track a little, but the other groups were saying, instead of banning you, we’re going to literally exile you, kill you, beat you. That’s right. Isn’t there a contrast here that’s worth noting? Yes, they mention this. Because it’s like, wait a minute. We may ban you from our congregation, but it’s not like we’re going to kill you. Whereas the Catholics and Protestants were actually doing that to the Anabaptists.

    That’s a great point, and I think it explicitly states that. When it deals with the sword and how they use it for punishment in a church-state situation, all we have for our maximum is to take you out of the church. That was a pretty big difference.

    38:56  |  Three: The Breaking of Bread

    So baptism was number one, ban is point number two, and point number three is the breaking of bread. And like I said, the order matters. You get into the church through baptism. The ban is to make a person aware of the need for discipline. And then the breaking of bread. The importance here is that you can tell from what they write that they don’t believe what the Catholics are saying. They’re not agreeing with transubstantiation. They don’t really get into detail about what they do mean, except that this is the body of Christ meeting together to break the bread and drink the wine, to remember what he’s done for us. So it could sound merely memorialist, and there’s no real wording that would say it’s only that. But the emphasis, once again, is that we have now been planted into the body of Christ, and we’re celebrating that.

    I felt the same thing. When you look at some of Sattler’s other records, particularly at the trial, you get a little more of this. But the Schleitheim Confession seems to be almost purposely vague on some of these nuances. And I like these confessions that say more of what it is and not what it’s not. It brings out this importance of the gathering. This idea of a pure gathering, trying, not perfect, but just trying to walk together in the resurrection. That precious time is what they had for their communion. I think that’s special.

    40:26  |  Four and Five: Separation and Shepherds

    So you had breaking of bread, and then that goes straight into the separation component.

    What is really clear is that everything is divided into two kingdoms. The good, the evil, the light, the darkness. And he just goes down through everything. His emphasis is on what is really the light and what is really the darkness. Let’s be willing to stand and be different. So separation means that we are nonconformed to the world.

    So they go from that point to what they call shepherds, or pastors, church leaders. What were they saying in that point of the Confession?

    Well, the most obvious thing is that once again we have order. It’s not just a free-for-all. They have order. And it even says what they expected the shepherds to do. One thing that’s implied but not specified is that we believe in the priesthood of the believers. And that’s been something Anabaptism, particularly conservative Anabaptism, has kept. It’s kind of surprising when I started coming into these circles, how you have common brothers, and then you go and be ordained, and suddenly you take that up. Everybody should be willing and getting to a place where they could be used for that purpose.

    41:56  |  Six and Seven: The Sword and the Oath

    That’s shepherds. And then it transitions to the next point, which is the sword. Talk me through that and its significance for us today.

    The vast majority of early Anabaptists followed through on what Conrad Grebel wrote, that we should be sheep, not people who pick up the sword, but sheep in this world. And then Schleitheim picks that right up with this section on the sword, and I think always refers back to Christ. Christ did this, or Christ said this.

    And what I think is one of the most significant things here is the way he sets up the two kingdoms, which I have hardly seen articulated this well since the early church. In the early church it’s very clearly stated that God did institute human government, and those are given to us from God. Romans 13 is there, and we cannot hide from Romans 13. The deeper understanding of that, however, when you look at the early church, is that the same sword the apostle Paul was speaking of in Romans 13 cut Paul’s head off. And so God uses these swords. He brings order into this world, but we are given a different calling in this world. You see that in the early church explicitly: when you said Jesus is Lord, it meant Caesar is not. And so it had huge implications. Jesus is our king. He’s the one we put our loyalty to.

    So we have separation from the world, which is one of the points. You have the sword, these different things that pull the Anabaptists back from what would have been mainstream expectations of society. And there’s another big one here, and that’s the oath. That’s the last one. It’s pushing us, as it says very clearly in Scripture, to make your yes, yes, and your no, no. And you can’t do any more than just say, I’ll do this if it’s possible.

    43:54  |  The Prison Letters to Horb

    Pretty directly after Schleitheim, Michael Sattler gets captured and put into prison. What happened from there? Where did that happen, and then what happens?

    It’s heading toward the end of his life. But it’s remarkable that in prison we have some really precious letters coming from Michael Sattler. It reveals a pastoral side of him that is impressive. He writes back to his local congregation, the church in Horb. And in there we get a few points he wants to make sure they understand. These are a bunch of people wanting to come together, live radical lives, encourage each other in holiness. And yet, how do you deal with discipline? So he encourages the church back there: yes, we want to be a disciplined church. But on the other hand, love has got to come out. And in that letter he says this: “Furthermore, dear fellow members in Christ, you should be admonished not to forget love, without which it is not possible for you to be a Christian congregation.”

    Isn’t that precious? In other words: you can have all the right answers, but if you have lost love, nothing counts. He’s quoting from 1 Corinthians 13. He goes on: “Therefore the shortcomings mentioned above can be removed and avoided. But if you love the neighbor, you will not scold or ban zealously.” You’re not going to overdo this. “Will not seek your own. Will not remember evil. Will not be ambitious or puffed up. But kind, righteous, generous in all gifts, humble and sympathetic with the weak and the imperfect.”

    I just love that. He’s gone through all these things. He’s not having this perfectionist, we’re all going to be perfect type of thing. We’re real people really coming together. And we’ve got to let love guide our fellowships. I just think that’s really precious.

    The other last thing he gives them, and this is a strong encouragement for all of us, comes from the parable in Luke chapter 19, of the talents. He says: “Remember the Lord who gave you a coin, that he will again require the same with interest. Lest that one talent be taken away from you, place it at interest according to the command of the Lord who entrusted it to you. I testify to you by the grace that you are vigilant, and that you walk as befits and becomes the saints of God. Give heed how the Lord rewards lazy servants, namely the lazy and tired hearts, clumsy and cold in the love of God and the brothers.” And he says, “Let no one shift your goal.” All through the time, and we have 500 years now. “Let no one shift your goal, which has been set in the letter by the Holy Scriptures, which is sealed by the blood of Christ and of many witnesses of Jesus.”

    47:10  |  Rottenburg: Making It Legal

    So we’re with Michael Sattler in prison, and he’s writing these letters from Horb, and they bring him here for the trial. Why was that? Who did they get to try him? I think that was the part, as I was reading about this, how much legalese is around this. It wasn’t just a lynching from a mob. There was a lot of procedure. Talk us through that process.

    Well, there were some who did want to have what we might call a lynching. There were some laws by the Swabian League that said you could just kill an Anabaptist. But the authorities, who were Austrian, and this is interesting, we’re pretty far from modern-day Austria, but this was Austrian back then. The Austrian authorities wanted it to be completely legal. And the problem was that in Horb there was a congregation that was Anabaptist, and the people who weren’t Anabaptists also tended to favor or like the Anabaptists. So it was a problem finding a place, and then getting legal authorities to come and hear the trial. Some of the people they asked at first, who were doctors of law, said no. And eventually they did find two doctors, doctors of the arts, to sit in and listen to the proceedings.

    It was a tense situation, because the people here, if they weren’t Anabaptists, still liked the Anabaptists. Sympathetic. Yes. Even the buildup of the drama in the area was a lot. When they moved him from Horb to here, it was with 24 horsemen. And then the Count here was worrying about a revolt, so he added 56 additional soldiers to go along with it. Imagine Michael Sattler and his wife and at least 13 others, with 24 horses and 56 soldiers. That’s quite a thing coming down the street.

    And I’m just thinking about the contrast. Here we are, it’s a nice beautiful summer day, there’s a cafe here now, it’s a nice square, people are going about their lives. But back then, almost 500 years from when we’re recording this, times were pretty different, at least in that particular situation. You can imagine that procession coming up that street, which I’m sure was here at the time. That would have been quite the spectacle, and also: whoa, something really serious is happening here. And we’re pretty far removed from that. It’s a little hard for me to get my head around, the contrast between that and what we’re experiencing right now.

    49:57  |  The Charges

    So the court here is epic in our Anabaptist history, in the way he was charged and that kind of thing. So I took the records here to discuss them a little back and forth. The date was May 17th, 1527. On the bench was Michael Sattler and his wife Margaretha and about 13, at least 13, others. So you can imagine, in a building pretty similar to this one that’s replaced it since, these charges being read aloud. A big one being going against the emperor, the charge of infant baptism, and then their view on the sacraments.

    So this is one of the charges from the clerk. Point number five says that they despised and scorned the mother of God and the saints. Michael Sattler’s answer to that was: “We do not dishonor the mother of God, for the mother of Christ is to be praised above all women, because God gave her the grace to give birth to the Savior of the whole world. And we do not revile the saints. But the Scriptures do not allow us to treat Mary or the saints as intercessors for us.”

    Point number six from the clerk: that he declared that one should not swear before a magistrate. He said simply, “The sixth charge is true, for swearing of oaths is forbidden by Christ himself.”

    Another of the charges, point number seven: that he has commenced a new and unheard of custom in regard to the Lord’s Supper, placing the bread and wine on a plate, eating and drinking of them. Can you give us more context on that charge? What did they think he was doing?

    Well, I’m assuming he’s following the procedure Conrad Grebel had given, which is: this is not transubstantiation. The bread and wine are not becoming the body and blood of Christ. So you don’t have this elaborate and special ceremony as in the Catholic Church. What he said was, serve it on a simple plate, and have someone without vestments, just one of the men in the group, share the bread and the wine. This sounds exactly like that.

    And so, just like the charge that you are dishonoring Mary: it seemed to them that if you won’t pray to Mary, you’re dishonoring her. And it seemed to them that if you just treat this wine and this bread as wine and bread, instead of as the consecrated body and blood of Christ, you’re dishonoring him. If you thought this really is the body and blood of Christ, that would be disrespectful.

    Another point here, again just quoting straight from the records we have. Point number eight: that contrary to the rule, he has married a wife. So give us some context on this. Well, read his reply. This response surprises me a little too. “As to my marriage, this is an ordinance of God.” Well, he did promise that he would be celibate. So you have to remember that. He is going against something he had promised earlier. The church did not always have celibate priests, but as the Middle Ages went on that became a requirement. So he’s going against what was expected. And I know they kind of winked at the fact that there were a lot of priests who had concubines. But he is saying, this is a good thing. So I think that’s the problem.

    53:20  |  The Turks

    The clerk, again, this is a quote: “He has said that if the Turks invaded the land, we ought not resist them. Does he approve of war?” This is a big one right here.

    His answer is very challenging. He says: “As regards the Turks, we will not fight them. For we are told in Scripture, thou shalt not kill. We are to beseech God with earnest prayers to repel and resist them. But if it were right for Christians to fight, I would rather go into battle against the so-called Christians who persecute, apprehend, and kill pious believers, than against the Turks. For the Turk is a genuine Turk and knows nothing of the Christian faith. But you claim to be Christians, boast of Christ, and still persecute the faithful witness of Christ. You are Turks according to the spirit.”

    Well, and you have to remember, the Turks had just crushed Hungary. They were threatening the empire. And if they captured Vienna, it would open the empire completely to their invasion. So this would sort of be like, when I was a young man, saying, I’m in favor of the Soviet Union and being taken over by them. Yes. And I can understand the feeling the non-Anabaptists had. But Sattler is saying that this is what God has called us to, to be non-resistant people in every situation.

    54:57  |  The Hangman Shall Dispute With You

    So it gets heated after that. Quote: “You wicked rogue, should we dispute with you? The hangman shall dispute with you.” And it feels like at this point the court, we don’t know for sure, devolves a bit from being a court proceeding to being: okay, we’re going to get you. We can’t believe you said that. You just crossed the line here. The hangman’s going to dispute with you. We know where this story is going to go.

    He makes this appeal now. He says, “Therefore, you ministers of God.” Now, I think that is a very interesting statement, how he’s referring to them. Just like the Schleitheim Confession: you ministers of God. He responds to that: “Therefore, you ministers of God, if you have not heard or read the Word of God, send for the most learned of your sacred books of the Bible and let them confer with us in the Word of God. If they prove to us by the Holy Scriptures that we err, we will gladly recant and suffer punishment. But if no error is found, I hope to God you will repent and receive instruction.”

    And then one of the replies to that statement is, quote, “It were better for you if you had never been born.” And he simply answers, “God knows what is good.”

    They continue to do a lot of mocking and threatening. Quote: “You arch-heretic, you have seduced the pious. If they would only now forsake their error.” And he simply answers, “Grace is with God alone.”

    And then one of the prisoners cries out, “We must not depart from the truth.” You can imagine the whole courtroom scene is getting a little heated. And you can see that in the response, because the next reply we have on record is, quote, “If there were no hangman here, I’d hang you myself, and I think I’d be doing God’s service.” At this point we don’t even need a conviction. We know where this is going to go.

    And the town clerk at this point turns to the judges and says, quote, “He will not stop today. Proceed with the sentence. I commit it to the law.” And finally, his reply to that was, “I am not sent to judge the Word of God in court. We are sent to bear witness of it. Therefore I cannot consent to any law unless shown otherwise by Scripture. I cannot be discharged. We are ready to suffer for the Word of God as long as breath remains in us.” And then again the clerk repeats what he has said before: “The hangman shall convince you, arch-heretic.” And he simply replies, “I appeal to the Scriptures.” And that’s it.

    57:33  |  Two Perspectives on Faithfulness

    We have two very different perspectives on what it means to be faithful. Again, it caused a kind of disruption even among the different non-Anabaptist people. Many of the key reformers, particularly those who knew him in Strasbourg, actually rose up and said, this is wrong.

    We were talking about how we’re in this place and it’s peaceful and all that. I really think the world is changing and has changed. And I think that doing what we’re doing is partly to waken us up. This could happen again. You are very right. And it might not look like it as we look at the people sitting around here with us. I think the things they’re committed to are shifting. And I think that as a people, we may become a rock of offense to them. So this is something important for us to think about. Amen.

    58:36  |  The Execution

    So we’re outside the city of Rottenburg now. Tell us about what happens next to Michael Sattler. The sentencing that comes down from the court: what happens to him at that point?

    Well, it was about a five minute drive to get out to this area, where it was known that they did these executions. And that path would have been gruesome. I’ll read a direct quote from the sentencing of what that would have been like, from the courthouse to here. Or from his prison house: it was a few days, and then he was taken here. It says, quote, “Michael Sattler shall be delivered to the executioner, who shall cut out his tongue, tear his body twice with red hot tongs on the wagon, and five more times beyond the city gate. Then he shall be burned to powder as a heretic.”

    And as a sign to his people who were here that this was bearable, he said he would raise two fingers. And when the cords holding him burned loose, he did do that. So the people were encouraged. He was staying faithful.

    And from here we know that Wilhelm Reublin, whose wife was one of those caught here, quickly published the account so people would know what was going on. And that caused a bit of a stir. But not only that: the public, and even the reformers he had a relationship with back in Strasbourg, when they heard about this, were very upset, and they wrote the authorities here complaining. And we have precious pieces of their letters that speak about their response. One is from Bucer. And Bucer was not known for just handing out compliments as a reformer there in Strasbourg. He said this in July of 1527: “We do not doubt that Michael Sattler, who was burned in Rottenburg, was a dear friend of God, although he was a leader of the Anabaptists, but much more skilled and honorable than some.”

    60:35  |  What Happened to Margaretha

    So what happened to Michael Sattler’s wife? She was part of this court trial as well. She had wanted to die with him, but they held her a few more days, and eventually they drowned her in the river nearby. And they tried to appeal to her, to get her to recant. There are some records that they actually worked on her for a while, but she stayed faithful. And there is some record that some of those women did recant. We hear that in the records. And she died by drowning, which they thought was the more merciful way to die, rather than burning at the stake.
